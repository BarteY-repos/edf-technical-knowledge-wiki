EDF6 native visibility: Umbra occlusion and measured frame costs

EDF 6Performance & 120 FPSPerformance
Technical reference · Exact-build graphics-worker analysis and scene-specific native-D3D11 runtime measurements

EDF6 uses Umbra visibility/culling inside graphics-worker jobs. A completion fence can account for much of a main-thread frame, but fence duration is waiting for queued work, not a single shader or occlusion-query measurement. The address map below applies to the studied EDF6 EDF.dll (PE timestamp 0x678CCB46, SHA-256 0d5092393a1b9d8f1b75b0c93d8a8f65615a36a92c46e40349c2b66c733d0916); other builds need renewed RE.

Native worker and visibility addresses

EDF.dll RVA / recordSource-derived behavior
0x11A305A → 0x111CC20Main-loop call to the synchronous xgl::gs::Device graphics-worker completion fence.
0x11A305F → 0x119FB40Immediately following cleanup: list-owned objects and nodes can be released; deferring the fence may invalidate their lifetime.
0x11B5A70Large callback family, renderer-state snapshot (60 payload records plus header); measured heavy path reaches Umbra::Camera::processVisibility.
0x11B5B30Related large state/visibility packet callback (61 payload records plus header); do not assume identical work cost.
0x1120B90Deferred one-payload reference/release/destruction callback family.
0x1127000Seven-payload resource/state binding and update callback family.
0x1126080, 0x11262F0Related state/resource callback families. Counting pending commands does not directly measure their executed cost.

The main thread may reach the fence while the graphics worker is still processing work already enqueued. A queue-depth count by itself cannot separate cheap pending callbacks from one expensive operation. Conversely, skipping the fence without proving callback/job ownership and the subsequent 0x119FB40 lifetime boundary can create use-after-release behavior.

Measured native D3D11 scene

The original experiment used an NVIDIA RTX 3060 Laptop, Microsoft D3D11/DXGI and 26 stable active observation windows (about 130 seconds). The following numbers are for that machine and scene only:

Metric Observation
Weighted Present frequency 59.318 Hz
First graphics fence 4.240 ms
Large visibility callback EDF+0x11B5A70 3.169 ms/call
Other measured callback 0.0562 ms/call
Umbra::Camera::processVisibility share of both callbacks 99.856%
Wait/GetData inside processVisibility 3.36% of its measured wall time

The dominance of processVisibility in these callbacks is not equivalent to an entire frame spending 99.856% in visibility. Other frame work was outside this measured pair.

Separate high-rate queue observation: in a different 20-window graphics-worker trace, 3,149 zero-fixed-update render frames spent a weighted 9.107 ms at the fence, while 5,992 frames containing a fixed update spent 5.452 ms; the whole-fence weighted average was 6.711 ms at 91.294 Presents/s. Entry queue depths were approximately 417.93 versus 408.04. This did not prove that a zero-step frame submits more expensive work: it can reach the fence sooner, allowing less main-thread/worker overlap. Do not merge these numbers with the independent 59.318-Hz NVIDIA experiment above.

Why removing the occlusion pass backfired

A controlled experiment in the same research disabled the native Umbra occlusion path. The result got worse, not better:

Measurement Native-occlusion baseline Occlusion disabled
Present rate 59.318 Hz 57.313 Hz
First graphics fence 4.240 ms 6.584 ms
processVisibility 2.387 ms/call 3.288 ms/call

With native culling removed, downstream visibility/rendering work and GPU queue depth could grow. It would be incorrect to conclude that the GPU was bottlenecked purely on waiting for an occlusion query. Similarly, reducing auxiliary visibility update cadence can produce black-surface flicker and delayed shadows in some scenes.

Main-world/type-0 and auxiliary/type-2 visibility producers are distinct (0x11B6AA0 and 0x11B6F90 respectively). The auxiliary producer operates over two persistent records of stride 0x150. Removing their update calls independently of Umbra state ownership caused stale or coarse visibility and shadows in the studied treatment. Skipping a required native resolve could also freeze level loading.

What remains unmeasured

Separate native instrumentation mapped internal processVisibility phases EDA0/F9C0, command families 0..12/END, atomic state and query pressure. Those probes had static/link validation, but no retained gameplay trace establishing which internal operation dominates. Consequently there is no supported general change to visibility cadence or fence behavior. These NVIDIA measurements also do not independently establish behavior on AMD hardware or DXVK.

A diagnostic design for rare multi-second Radeon stalls measures Present blocking, the first fence, seven worker callback families, queue counters and adapter VRAM separately. Its existence is not evidence that a specific Radeon driver or VRAM-residency cause was confirmed. In any such analysis, a long Present with a short fence points to a different subsystem than a long fence accompanied by a long 0x11B5A70 callback.

Evidence and scope: originally measured EDF6 visibility experiment P1/P2 and source-identified Umbra routines. Results were captured in a specific modded diagnostic environment; they are not a claim about all stock installations or an endorsed plugin configuration.
