EDF6 moving surfaces: why standing soldiers do not inherit platform motion

EDF 6Game technical referencesCharacter motion
Technical reference · Build-scoped static analysis; original contributor evidence grades cited; no new R126 gameplay runtime

The stock EDF6 walking controller asks Havok whether its character body is supported. That query includes both surface linear velocity and surface angular velocity. In the studied implementation, the subsequent walking step uses the support state and normal, but does not add those reported surface velocities to the character's own movement.

Static code consequence

The original Walk_Step final velocity is assembled from the walk controller's motion channels, not by appending surfaceVelocity. A soldier standing on a sideways-moving body therefore tends to retain the character's own world velocity while the supporting body moves beneath it. Physics contacts can still push the soldier up with a rising surface or sideways with an intersecting wall. The source derives these results from code; it does not claim a new systematic gameplay measurement for every platform type.

The contact surface itself may be a moving vehicle part, a keyframed ragdoll body, a crate/container or an animated event object. The native collision table matters: the player movement layer 1 contacts layers 1, 6–9, 14–20, 25 and 26, but not all enemy movement or flight layers. For example, a helicopter's keyframed component might be supportable while its distinct flight body is not — that specific vehicle interpretation is source inference.

Why using Havok's returned surface velocity is not trivial

An earlier external moving-platform investigation found Havok's reported surfaceVelocity was zero on some EDF vehicle contacts. The contributor then resolved the supporting rigid body and computed point velocity from the body's actual motion, including angular velocity. This is useful diagnostic evidence about which field holds the relevant motion, not evidence that the original game already incorporates it.

This distinction is especially important for standing on animated bosses, vehicles and environmental objects: their keyframed body positions may move visibly even when the controller's reported support velocity is not used by gameplay movement.
